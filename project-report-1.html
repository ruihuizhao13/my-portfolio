Project Overview

The ELL Writing Center is a working prototype that logs how English-language-learner (ELL) graduate students plan, draft, monitor and revise their academic writing. It turns those behavioral traces into SRSD-aligned strategy prompts for the student and a reasoning guide for the tutor. Every metric traces back to a specific component of self-regulated learning.

Problem Statement & Context

ELL graduate students write seminar papers, theses and journal articles while carrying four compounding challenges. Institutional support is mostly reactive: a student brings a draft, gets impressionistic feedback and leaves. Nothing in that loop shows how the student actually planned or revised.

Key Challenge

How can learning analytics make the writing process visible and actionable, both for the students themselves and for the tutors who support them?

Language load

Managing academic English syntax and vocabulary while building complex disciplinary arguments.

Genre unfamiliarity

Argumentation, hedging and citation conventions are rarely taught explicitly to non-native writers.

Identity & self-efficacy

Students who doubt their academic voice revise less, avoid risk and settle for shallow edits.

Reactive-only support

No systematic strategy instruction; help arrives only after a draft exists.

The Learners: A Triple Burden

The target learners sit where three pressures meet. Many are first-generation international students with strong content knowledge but unfamiliar conventions. They are English-language learners. And they write at a graduate level, where stakes are high and scaffolding is thin. Their writing shows consistent, detectable signals, which became the starting point for what the tool measures:

L1 transfer patterns Informal register Citation gaps Limited lexical variety

Theoretical Framework

Two frameworks decide what gets logged. Harris and Graham's SRSD model gives the instructional sequence, moving learners from background knowledge through modeling to independent strategy use (I do → We do → You do). Zimmerman's Self-Regulated Learning cycle maps onto those stages and gives a vocabulary for choosing what to log, so the analytics are theoretically grounded rather than data-driven for their own sake.

SRSD six-stage model (Harris & Graham, 1996) mapped onto the SRL cycle (Zimmerman, 2000). The tool also builds in SRSD's PLANS and REVISE mnemonics, self-monitoring checklists and performance graphing.

Analytics Design

From Keystrokes to an Event Log

Each action in the writing workspace is captured as one discrete event in a 12-column log. Pauses between actions are measured automatically, because long pauses signal planning. Character counts are positive for additions and negative for deletions, which makes revision depth computable. Every field maps to an SRSD construct and can be collected from ordinary digital writing tools.

If-Then Rules Turn Metrics into Instruction

Five process metrics are computed from the raw log. When one crosses its threshold, the system surfaces a targeted, SRSD-aligned intervention for the student or alerts the tutor.

PlanningScore

# SetGoal + PlanOutline
IF < 2

Prompt the student to set explicit, measurable goals before drafting.

TranslationFluency

words ÷ writing minutes
IF < 8 wpm

Suggest freewriting in the first language, then translating core ideas.

RevisionScore

|Σ negative charCount|
IF < 20

Alert the tutor to likely surface-only edits; introduce the REVISE mnemonic.

MetacogScore

# CheckRubric + Highlight + SelfEvaluate
IF = 0

Surface the rubric checklist and prompt one self-assessment step.

SocialSupportScore

# RequestFeedback + ReceiveFeedback
IF 0 across 3 sessions

Recommend peer review or a writing center appointment.

Writing-quality outcomes sit alongside the process metrics: word count, type-token ratio for lexical diversity, average sentence length, and grammar errors from an external NLP checker.

The Prototype

I built the design as a working single-page prototype so the analytics logic could be tested against real interaction instead of only being described. It runs entirely in the browser. Open the demo, pick a student, and every click and keystroke is logged.

One Log, Read by the Tutor

The same data becomes a tutor reasoning guide. Each recommendation traces back to an SRSD-grounded threshold, so the tutor knows what to do next session and why.

Reflection & Next Steps

Key Learnings

  • Starting from theory made the data model easier to defend: every logged field answers "which SRL component does this show?"
  • Thresholds are only useful when each one is paired with a specific instructional move.
  • The same event log can serve different people if each view is phrased for what that person needs to do next.
  • Building a clickable prototype exposed design questions a slide deck could not, such as how to estimate writing time from pauses.

Next Steps

Pilot the prototype with ELL graduate students and writing center tutors, check whether the thresholds match tutors' own judgments, and add an institution-level view so program staff can see patterns across students and sessions.
